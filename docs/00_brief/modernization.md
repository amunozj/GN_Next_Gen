This document describes a problem of data homogenization that currently is not maximizing the use of all information available.  It involves the calibration of the group sunspot number across centuries.

The challenge is that not all observers count the same number of groups and the relationship between counts is not well understood.   Not all days are observed, and those with observations may or may not have multiple observers looking at the same time.  Additionally, the observational pattern of observers reflects the weather, and there may be an undercount of spotless days (i.e. when the Sun is spotless, observers may not report it).   Furthermore, There may be a drift in visual accuity with observer age, slight differences on what to consider a group, difference in telescope magnification, whether the observer is using a projection upon a screen or directly seeing the Sun through a filter, differences in the ability of observers to separate groups during solar maximum vs. quieter times.

There are two approaches to trying to calibrate these observers in time:

1. Active Day Fraction Calibration: Try to estimate the observational threshold of each observer by comparing the distribution of monthly active day fractions against a refernce survey like RGO.  Once this threshold is identified, a calibration curve can be estimated using RGO against itself.   This method indepenly connects all observers with RGO and can be applied even when there is no overlap.  The problem is that this threshold and calibration curves will change depending on the reference period/cycle, and handles mising observational patterns poorly.   This repository aims to address that, by imposing the observational missing day pattern on RGO and runing a sliding window of the size as that of the observer being calibrated over the whole RGO period.   The issue we currently have is that we don't have a proper way of assessing uncertanty, we are not taking advantage of overlapping observer information, and we are not taking advantage of sunspot emerging patterns in time, which we know to be different for different activity levels because all this gets folded into a single fraction per month. 2. Backbone method: This method uses observers that have overlap in time to identify the relationship between sunspot group counts in observers that look at the Sun at same time.  Not all observers have overlap, thus the calibration happens by choosing core observers that connect through the entire observation period and matching overlapping obsersvers to them.  This requires a careful propagation of error and suffers from the problem that there are periods with little overlap and sketchy connections.  Inside this folder: C:\Users\amunoz\OneDrive - SWRI\Documents\AAResearch\Codes\Dynamo\Code in Exp4\Sunspot Data\GN Series is a codebase that explores connecting observers as graph network and defines the distance based on the linearity of the relationship between their counts and minimizes a distance defined by error propagation as a pathfinding problem.   It aims to connect the past and the present as well as possible.  This aimed to be an alternative/improvement over human-chosen backbones.  The problem still remains as to how to chose the best paths and how to properly quantify the uncertainty associated with that choice.

By far the hardest period to work with is the Maunder Minimum (~1645-1715) which has very observations, some of them are suspect spotless days, and there is both very little overlap between observers and no real matching modern RGO cycles that can be used to calibrate active day fractions.  Observers of the period often report years without spots in blanket statements, but there is no way of turning that into specific dates of observation.

Neither of these methods is taking advantage of the fact that groups/sunspots have extended lifetimes to fill gaps and/or connect observers that have observations within days of each other.  Neither method properly takes advantage of redundacy: e.g. that many observers are supposed to be looking and the same sun and that the real number of groups is knowable latent.  Neither method is taking advantage of modern prior+evidence methods.
